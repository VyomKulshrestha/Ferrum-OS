#### Summary

The paper asks what happens when attacks, or an attacker, fall inside the part of the score range from which an adaptive alarm threshold learns. Three self-registered studies (tt1, tt1b, tt1d) on six datasets compare a frozen threshold with a symmetric quantile tracker, daily recalibration, SPOT/DSPOT, a tighten-only rule, ceilings and several variants. On recorded attacks the tracker detects fewer events than the frozen threshold on HAI 20.07 (20 against 36 of 38, RP), BATADAL (6 against 14 of 14, RP component; the tt1b primary rule failed through H3) and on Tennessee Eastman "followers" (66 against 104 of 104, RP component H1a; the tt1d primary rule failed through H1b). Under a count that requires an alarm to start inside the attack the loss is not significant on HAI 20.07 (PH) or Tennessee Eastman (RP, failed) and remains on BATADAL (PH); ProvICS shows no loss (RP, failed). Score-level campaigns on 56 sealed attack-free Tennessee Eastman runs (RH, H4 to H6 all passed) show that loud pulses, sub-event pulses and a closed-loop "frog" each blind one family of rules. Four propositions give the tracker's memory, the daily rule's memory, and by-construction guarantees for tighten-only and ceiling rules. The paper recommends that thresholds tighten automatically and loosen only under a ceiling or with operator approval.

#### Strengths

1. The reporting is unusually candid. The paper states that three of four primary success rules failed, that G1 and H3 were uninformative, that the HAI 20.07 gap depends on crediting standing alarms, and that ProvICS needed an amendment. I read the three protocols and amendment 1 and the paper's account of them is accurate.
2. The numbers trace to the record. I compared against the result files every cell of Tables 3 to 12 and 17, two of the three columns of Table 16, the abstract, and the text numbers of Sections 1, 3.1, 5, 6, 7.2 to 7.5 and 7.7 (more than 400 values). I found one mismatch, a descriptive one (BATADAL test length, see W14 and the mismatch list). Rounding is correct throughout.
3. The sealed attack-free Tennessee Eastman runs fix the defect of the earlier campaign tests: controls can now pass or fail (adaptive controls 44 to 51 of 56 at 1.05 tau0, RS), one placement per run, hypotheses fixed in advance.
4. Proposition 1 is a useful observation for practitioners: a quantile tracker's recovery time is E(1-p)/p steps whatever its step size, so at alarm-grade p (7.2e-4 per second on HAI 20.07, registered calibration value) one minute of exceedance costs 23 hours. Slowing the tracker does not help. The post-hoc check on trajectories agrees.
5. The "learning region" view is a clean way to organise attacks on threshold rules, and the sub-event pulse attack on rules that exclude alarm episodes is a neat consequence of it.

**W5. The registered campaigns are written into the score. The paper does not say clearly, where it makes the claims, what an attacker must have.** (judgement c)
Evidence checked. The tt1d protocol defines the campaigns as s_t = max(s_t, 0.97 x deployed threshold), max(s_t, 2 tau0) and max(s_t, 2 tau_t), with terminal "attacks" that are 600 s score bumps of m tau0, and states twice that these are "upper bounds on a physical attacker". The paper says so in Section 3 (A2), Section 6.3 ("at the score level") and Section 9. The abstract does not: "a day of two-minute nuisance alarms left the tracker catching none of 56 later, just-detectable attacks" reads as nuisance alarms and attacks on a plant. The second introduction bullet has the same problem.
My assessment as an ICS reviewer. (i) The score is an internal quantity of the monitor (top-3 standardised residuals of a ridge plus LightGBM model over 41 measurements, summed over two horizons and smoothed). To hold it at 0.97 tau_t an attacker needs the model, its residual scales, the live inputs of every sensor and actuator, and the current threshold state. The threshold lives in the monitor; an attacker who can read it there can usually silence the monitor. "By probing" means observing alarms, and each probe that finds the threshold is an alarm. (ii) An attacker who can set the score needs no poisoning: he sets it below the threshold during the terminal attack. So the score-level result is an upper bound on a capability that makes the attack unnecessary. The interesting question is whether poisoning is cheaper than concealment for a sensor-level attacker, and Section 3 says "We did not compare the two strategies". The abstract's "Deliberate poisoning is cheaper" has no cost measure behind it. (iii) The terminal attack is defined in detector units. Nothing shows that a 600 s excursion at 1.05 to 1.5 tau0 corresponds to process harm. Tennessee Eastman is a simulator with shutdown limits, so this could be shown. (iv) The registered frog moved the standard SPOT to a median 1.75 tau0 after 24 h (descriptive value in the registered file; minimum 1.19, one numerical runaway), and SPOT still caught 32 of 56 bumps at 2 tau0 (RS, not in the paper). The "five orders of magnitude" runaway is EX on HAI only. On ProvICS the frog moved SPOT by 0.6 % (descriptive P4, failed).
Status: ANSWERED in part (Sections 3, 9). OPEN in the abstract and introduction, and no capability statement exists.
Fix: A, then C. A: add a short attacker-capability table to Section 3 (write access to what, read access to what, model knowledge, dwell time, alarms raised during the campaign) for A0, A1, score-level A2 and sensor-level A2; put "score-level" into the abstract and the two introduction bullets; delete "cheaper". (iii) Where loosening was needed, the recommended rules did not deliver it. (iv) "Or with approval" is advice, not a result. The quiet-log gate caught 1 of 56 (6 h review) and 32 of 56 (daily review) bumps at 1.05 tau0 after the frog against 44 in control (RS). The oracle permit caught 1 of 10 in the G1 controls (RS). No human was tested.

#### Summary

The paper asks what happens when the part of the score stream that an adaptive alarm threshold learns from (its "learning region") contains attacks. Three self-registered studies compare a frozen threshold with a symmetric quantile tracker, daily recalibration, SPOT/DSPOT and several hardened variants on six fresh datasets (three spent HAI releases serve for development). The tracker detects fewer labelled attacks than the frozen threshold under an "any alarm inside the event" count on HAI 20.07 (20 vs 36 of 38, registered primary gate G2), BATADAL (6 vs 14 of 14, registered primary component H1 of a failed primary rule) and Tennessee Eastman follower events (66 vs 104 of 104, registered primary component H1a of a failed primary rule). Under a stricter onset count the loss disappears on HAI 20.07 (post-hoc) and Tennessee Eastman (registered primary component H1b, failed) and remains on BATADAL (post-hoc). Score-level campaigns on 56 sealed attack-free Tennessee Eastman runs blind the tracker, an event-excluding recalibration and a maximum-likelihood SPOT (H4–H6, registered non-primary hypotheses, all passed). Four propositions give the tracker's memory and the trivial recall guarantee of a threshold that never rises above its calibrated value. The paper recommends that thresholds tighten automatically and loosen only under a ceiling or with operator approval.

I checked the paper against the protocol and result files with my own code. The reporting is unusually honest and almost every body number I traced is reproduced exactly. My objections are about what the numbers mean: the headline metric has no chance baseline, the confirmatory weight of the "registered" campaign results is much lower than the word suggests, the one dataset on which the loss survives the stricter count uses a tracker whose step is 6.9 times the threshold, and the defence guidance is supported mainly by a tautology.

#### Strengths

1. **Traceable and candid reporting.** Of the body numbers I traced to `results\` (Tables 3–10, the abstract, and the text of Sections 3.1, 5 and 6 apart from the items listed under "What I did not check"), one sentence disagrees with the files (W17). Failed primary rules are named in contribution 1, Table 6, Section 6.2, Section 6.4 and the first Limitations bullet. The authors volunteer an exact bound that undermines their own H2 on BATADAL.
2. **H1a on Tennessee Eastman is a real out-of-sample prediction.** The protocol states that the real-event hypotheses could not be rehearsed; the unit of resampling is the run (`real_events.D1.resampling_unit = "run"`), which is the correct unit; the effect is large (38 discordant events, all favouring frozen; registered).
3. **Self-critical measurement.** The time-in-alarm and onset-count analysis (Section 5.2, post-hoc) weakens the authors' own G2 result and is reported at length; ProvICS (P1 failed) and the uninformative G1/H3 are kept.
4. **The learning-region framing is a useful organising idea**, and the sub-event pulse attack on a recalibration that excludes alarm episodes is a neat observation that event-exclusion merely moves the attack surface.
5. **The onset caveat in Proposition 3** is stated rather than hidden.

#### Weaknesses

Each item gives the evidence I checked, whether the paper already answers it, and the class of fix: (A) writing / framing / analysis of existing result files, (B) a new run on spent data, to be labelled exploratory, (C) new data.

#### Summary

The paper asks what happens when attacks, or an attacker, land in the part of the score range from which an adaptive alarm-threshold rule learns. Three registered studies (tt1, tt1b, tt1d) on six datasets compare a frozen threshold with trackers, daily recalibration, SPOT/DSPOT and several hardened variants. Under an any-alarm count the symmetric tracker detects fewer labelled events than the frozen threshold on HAI 20.07, BATADAL and Tennessee Eastman followers (all registered primary components); under an onset count the difference survives on BATADAL only. Score-level campaigns on 56 sealed attack-free Tennessee Eastman runs (registered non-primary H4-H6) blind the tracker, an episode-excluding recalibration and maximum-likelihood SPOT to small terminal bumps. Four short propositions give the tracker's memory and the by-construction guarantees of tighten-only and ceiling rules. The paper recommends that thresholds tighten freely and loosen only under a ceiling or with operator approval.

I found no number in the body that disagrees with the result or protocol files. My objections concern what the numbers mean, how the claims are worded, the threat model, and density.

#### Strengths

1. Reporting discipline is well above the norm for this area: failed primary rules, an uninformative gate, a failed first attempt and its amendment, superseded post-hoc analyses and dropped hypotheses are all in the paper.
2. Every number I traced matched its file (list under "What I checked"). Tables 3-10 and Appendix E reproduce the files, including unfavourable rows.
3. The abstract already carries the main caveat (the loss does not survive the onset count on HAI 20.07 or Tennessee Eastman), which most papers would bury.
4. Proposition 1 is simple and useful: recovery time depends on the calibrated exceedance rate, not on the step size. The post-hoc integrality check (largest deviation 7e-6 of a step) supports the implementation.
5. tt1d fixes real defects of tt1/tt1b: both counts registered, campaign controls that can fail, an evaluability rule, sealed attack-free runs, resampling of whole runs.
6. Propositions 3-4 give a guarantee that does not depend on any dataset, and the verifier checks it on every event (0 violations).

## Part 4. Meta-review (step 4 of the prompt)

**Decision: major revision.** All three reviewers score 2 (weak reject) with confidence 4. As it stands the paper would not be accepted, and in a cycle with no revision outcome the decision is reject and encourage resubmission. The committee recommends revision and not rejection because the record behind the paper is sound, the errors found are few and small, and the changes that would move each reviewer to 3 are mostly writing and tabulation of files that exist. R1 would reach weak accept with the reframing, the robust baselines and half the exploratory material moved out, and accept with a process-level campaign. R2 would support a narrower paper once the primary record, the chance baseline, the mode-level campaign result and the guidance are stated as the evidence has them. R3 would move to 3 with the reframing, three small tables, the cuts and two exploratory runs.

### Consensus

**What the committee values.**
- **Reporting discipline.** Failed primary rules, uninformative gates, a failed first attempt with its amendment, superseded analyses and dropped hypotheses are all in the paper. Across three independent traces the body numbers agree with the result files, with the five exceptions of Part 2, A.
- **tt1d repaired real defects of the earlier studies:** both detection counts registered, campaign controls that can pass or fail, an evaluability rule, sealed attack-free runs, whole runs resampled.
- **Proposition 1 in operational terms.** Recovery per exceedance step depends on the calibrated exceedance rate and not on the step size; the post-hoc trajectory check agrees.
- **The learning-region view,** and the observation that leaving alarm episodes out of the update moves the attack surface to pulses shorter than an event.

**What the registered record supports.**
1. A symmetric quantile tracker stops alarming during a sustained attack and stays raised afterwards. Registered under the any-alarm count on HAI 20.07 (20 against 36 of 38 **[reg-P]**), BATADAL (6 against 14 **[reg-P]**) and the Tennessee Eastman noise attacks (57 against 95 **[reg-S]**; followers 66 against 104 **[reg-P]**). It is what Proposition 1 predicts.
2. Under the onset count the loss is established on BATADAL only: 14 attacks, a post-hoc count, a step of 6.9 τ0.
3. At the score level, on seven Tennessee Eastman monitors, each family of self-loosening rules can be driven upward through its learning region (H4 to H6 **[reg-H]**), and a ceiling confines the effect by construction (Proposition 4; H3 is an implementation check).
4. The frozen threshold was inside its false-alarm budget on three of eight evaluations, all **[reg-S]**: the attack-free Tennessee Eastman runs (0.98), BATADAL (0.70) and 13 h of attack-free ProvICS rows (0.92). It exceeded the budget on the Tennessee Eastman attack runs (1.55), HAI 20.07 (1.11, **[reg-P]**), HyGrid-ICS (9.6), voraus-AD (1.62) and the ProvICS attack recording (33).

**What would sink the paper in its current form** (each verified in Part 2).
1. **The headline says "attacks let through"; the registered evidence says "the alarm cleared".** On Tennessee Eastman every lost event is a later phase of a noise attack the tracker had already alarmed on, and the two rules detect the same number of attack runs (B1). On HAI 20.07 the gap depends on standing alarms, as the paper says. The one onset-robust loss rests on a mis-scaled tracker (B4). All three reviewers put this first.
2. **The any-alarm count has no chance baseline** (B2). On half of the Tennessee Eastman first events the frozen threshold's detections are about what its false-alarm rate gives. That weakens H1c, the "First" column of Table 8 and the only measured benefit of tightening. R1 and R2 found this independently, as did the chair.
3. **The abstract understates the failed primaries and overstates three claims** (B3, B8; R3 W4): one of three failed primary rules is named; "Deliberate poisoning is cheaper" has no cost measure behind it, and Section 3 says the strategies were not compared; "cannot miss an event the frozen threshold catches" drops Proposition 3's any-alarm qualifier; "ran at 0.98 times its false-alarm budget" is one of the three evaluations, out of eight, on which the frozen threshold was inside its budget.
4. **The campaigns are not yet a threat model** (B5, B6). They write the score; the paper nowhere states in one place what the attacker must read and write; two of three are alarm floods by the standards the paper cites; the frog is silent only to its victim rule; the sub-event attacker needs the live threshold; the 56 placements are seven deterministic monitor-level outcomes that a rehearsal had already shown; ProvICS showed no frog effect **[reg-D]**; the sensor-level variant is twelve exploratory placements on replayed data.
5. **The guidance goes beyond the evidence** (B7). "Never let a threshold rise above a fixed ceiling" follows by construction. "Tighten freely" costs 1.2 to 3.5 times the frozen threshold's false-alarm episodes (B7: 2.43 against 0.70, 1.57 against 0.98, 1.81 against 1.55 **[reg-S]**) for no demonstrated gain. Every approval mechanism tested failed. Where the frozen threshold did not hold (ProvICS, HAI 20.07) the recommended rules gave no relief, and the remedy the paper names for that case, offline recalibration on verified clean data, was never run.
6. **Twelve pages hold too much.** Three studies, nine datasets, twenty rules, three scores, four propositions, ten tables and three figures. Tables print a page behind their text. A reader cannot keep each claim's status in mind, and that status is the paper's main selling point.

**Disagreements between reviewers.** None on direction. R1 gives most weight to the missing deployment evidence and robust baselines (W8) and to plant feedback in the sensor-level frog (W6); R2 to the chance baseline, the mode-level sample size and the exact bounds; R3 to wording and to the frozen threshold as a baseline under drift. R1 counts Proposition 1 as a strength for practitioners; R2 holds that Propositions 2 to 4 should be remarks and that "23 hours" describes a condition (exceedance-free data) that the recordings never met.

### The five questions of the prompt

- **Do the abstract and introduction claim more than the registered outcomes support?** Yes, in specific places, though the abstract is more candid than most. The title, "lost attacks that a frozen threshold caught" for the Tennessee Eastman followers, the single named primary failure, "Deliberate poisoning is cheaper", the unqualified tighten-only guarantee, "none of the rules we tested that can raise their own threshold is safe", and the 0.98 budget figure.
- **Can a reader tell the status of every body number?** At table level, yes: every results table carries a status. In running text, no. ### (c) Changes that need new data

1. **A process-level campaign with a consequence measure.** Poisoning injected at sensors or setpoints in a closed-loop Tennessee Eastman simulation, with the terminal attack measured by process consequence (for example approach to a shutdown limit) and compared with direct concealment by the same attacker. This is new simulated data, not the spent dataset. R1 and R3 name it as the step from an upper bound to a result.
2. **A registered sensor-level injection on the 53 reserved attack-free Tennessee Eastman runs,** if the later study they are reserved for is this one. Define it after (b8), register it with an external timestamp, and take the mode as the unit of analysis.
3. **SWaT and WADI** under the existing tt1b registration when access is granted. They would add testbed plants to a record whose positive results are all on simulated or hardware-in-the-loop systems.
4. **A recording with benign drift and attacks,** on which a frozen threshold demonstrably loses its budget and offline recalibration can be compared with the adaptive rules. The guidance concerns exactly this case, and no dataset in the paper tests it cleanly: on HAI 20.07 and ProvICS the frozen threshold lost its budget, but the paper has no recalibration baseline and the cause is not established.
5. **A human approval study,** or at least operator-in-the-loop evidence from the literature, for recommendation 3.
6. **Evidence of deployment:** which ICS monitors or products adapt their alarm threshold online, and how.
